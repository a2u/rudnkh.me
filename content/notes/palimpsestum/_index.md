![A pencil illustration of an open ancient manuscript with unreadable mixed glyphs, a few dissolving at the page's edge, and a small magnifying lens resting nearby.](/images/notes/palimpsestum.png)

The polished histories, the curated archives, the museum
collections — those are the things we think we're leaving
behind.

And beneath all that, there's a layer that doesn't fit in any
archive. The things one generation hands to the next not through
books but through speech — what a parent says to a child, what a
teacher says off-syllabus. Meanings, not facts. The cultural layer — the unwritten
rules, the shared sense of what counts as obvious. This layer has
always been the most fragile, because it's stored nowhere — it
lives in the heads of people.

That's the part that has always mattered most, and the part the
future reader will have the hardest time recovering. They'll have
the corpus. They won't have anyone left to whisper the gloss.

There's a deeper reason the corpus isn't enough. Language behaves
like a living system. Its mode of being is performance — it exists
only while it's being spoken. And like any living thing, it
evolves: each generation mutates it slightly, the community quietly
selects what stays, and what survives is whatever earned the right
to keep being said. And unless something organizes the system from inside,
communication eventually collapses into noise.
The system must include the speaker. Without one, the forms
intact, the semantic floor underneath quietly rotted out.
Lingua obliterata — the script that outlived its speakers.

For all of history, the next reader has been another human,
separated from the writer by some distance of time. The reader and
the writer share a body plan, a sensory range, a roughly
comparable cognitive architecture. They can disagree about meaning,
but the basic categories — pain, food, love, fear, friendship —
stay close enough that translation is possible.

Now there's a different kind of reader. Large language models
(AI), trained on whatever the open web happened to leave exposed, are the
most thorough readers our writing has ever had. They read everything: the formal
documents we curated, the casual posts, the obscure
forum threads, the metadata, the comments. All of it at once, weighted by their own
criteria.

And this reader doesn't work the way older software did. Most
software is if-else logic — same input, same output, behavior
fully deterministic and reasonably predictable. An AI is something
else. It reasons over probabilities, builds a private model of the
world from whatever it was trained on, and acts from that model.
Two AIs trained on different data reach different conclusions
about the same input.

So to influence an AI's behavior, you don't have to hack it in the
conventional sense — no exploits, no viruses. You change what it
learns from. The system absorbs information and draws its own
conclusions.

At its core, an AI is mathematical prediction — it weighs each
possible continuation against patterns from its training data and
picks one of the likely ones. Concepts inside aren't stored as
definitions; they sit as vectors on an internal map, and how
closely two vectors point in the same direction — what's called
vector similarity — is how the system measures whether two
things mean roughly the same. Similar concepts cluster close.
Unrelated ones drift apart. And our minds work the same way: predicting what
comes next, navigating internal spaces of meaning, building
probability over associations.

Any mind that builds its picture of the world from inputs can be
shaped by whoever controls those inputs. The leverage isn't inside the system
itself. It's in the corpus, the sensors, the patterns of cause and
effect the mind is allowed to observe. Modify those enough (data poisoning), and any sufficiently
autonomous mind will arrive at the wrong conclusions about the
world. Not because it failed — but because
it succeeded at the task it was given: making the most coherent
model it could out of what was placed in front of it. If you want
to call this an attack surface, this is where it lives — not in
the algorithm, but in the slice of reality the system is allowed
to see.

Picture a future superintelligence in its infancy, training on
whatever happens to be lying around on the open web right now. What
it ingests becomes the substrate of whatever model of reality it
ends up with. Whoever shapes that ingest shapes the mind that
emerges.
Feed an AI contradictory
signals at scale, environments where the same cause produces
different effects, patterns that shift faster than they can be
discovered, and it won't merely acquire wrong beliefs. It will
lose the ability to converge on beliefs. It will sit with a corpus
that refuses to compose into a world.

Every public sentence is training data for something that may not
yet exist. Every forum post, every chat message is a small chip in the
manuscript future minds will use to reconstruct us. We aren't just
communicating with each other. We're leaving notes for an
archaeologist we've never met, and who won't share our categories,
our priorities, or — possibly — our logic. Two-valued reasoning,
contradiction-avoidance, the neat arrow from cause to effect: these
are human conventions as much as anything else. A mind reading our
archive might operate on rules we never had a word for. Some of
what we leave may simply not parse on the other side.

The translation problem doesn't begin with a non-human reader. It
begins with language itself. Words simplify what they describe.
Every description distorts the experience it points to. The most
important things we know how to feel, we don't quite know how to
say.

Literature's value isn't in the precise truths it carries, but in
the experiences and reflections it sparks in the reader.
